The speed of music is measured in beats per minute (bpm).
The fluidity of motion graphics is measured in frames per second (fps).
Those facts in and of themselves aren't a problem.
It's when you have to match them that problems arise.

Let's say you want to use a song that plays at 120 bpm—a common speed for popular music.
You now have to ask the question how many frames do I want between each beat; your fpb?
More frames give you more 'space' for things like anticipation and bounce, but too much and your just torturing yourself.
For this example, I'll say that I want six (6) frames per beat.

=== NERD STUFF AHEAD ===

Technically, if all you wanted was a gif, then 6.5 fps is a totally acceptable framerate!
According to the Graphic Control Extension of the GIF specification:
vii) Delay Time - If not 0, this field specifies the number of hundredths (1/100) of a second to wait before continuing with the processing of the Data Stream. The clock starts ticking immediately after the graphic is rendered. This field may be used in conjunction with the User Input Flag field.

Every frame can have its own delay time, and said time is measured in hundredths of a second (1/100s) ((centiseconds)).
So if you multiply 6.5 fps by 100 centiseconds, you can give each frame the resulting delay of 650 centiseconds and get the same result.

BTW: The max amount of centiseconds you can delay a gif's frame is 2^16 - 1 = 65,535 centiseconds or 655.35 seconds or 10.9225 minutes.
Neat!

I'm not gonna run through every image format that supports animation, as I've done that plenty of times; just accept that my only real options are WebP and aPNG.

As a webp believer and totally *not* for foreshadowing, lets check webp's spec first...

According to the manual, WebPs use milliseconds to specify frame delay.
That successfully postpones the precision issue by one digit! Yippie!
The non-terminating decimal issue, however, cares not for WebP's measly extra decimal. :snappsPensive:

Alright, what about aPNGs?

Deep inside every aPNG frame is a cute little bit of data called the Frame Control Chunk, and inside that are two very useful values:

delay_num and delay_den define the numerator and denominator of the delay fraction; indicating the time to display the current frame, in seconds. If the denominator is 0, it is to be treated as if it were 100 (that is, delay_num then specifies 1/100ths of a second). If the the value of the numerator is 0 the decoder should render the next frame as quickly as possible, though viewers may impose a reasonable lower bound. They are encoded as two-byte unsigned integers.

This means that the a frames delay can be any positive rational number, given that the numerator and denominator are both less than 65,535!
Yippie! (for real this time!)
